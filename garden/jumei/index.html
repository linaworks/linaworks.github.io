The Rise and Fall of Jumei

Business · Growing · Planted October 2026

In May 2014, Jumei went public on the NYSE at $22 per ADS. Less than six years later, it went private.

Jumei started as an online beauty retailer, selling cosmetics through curated selections and promotional deals. It wasn't a small business that never found its market. In 2013, it had roughly 10.5 million active customers, made $483 million in revenue and $25 million in net income, and held an estimated 22.1% of China's online beauty market by GMV, according to its IPO prospectus.

"It grew fast, then faded" isn't an explanation. I want to understand what happened in between.

What the numbers show

2016 stands out.

Orders fell faster than the customer count. That could mean fewer purchases per active customer, depending on how Jumei counted both.

Jumei's annual report points to China's April 2016 change in import taxes on cross-border e-commerce, which hurt sales at Jumei Global.

The policy change mattered. But how much of the decline did it explain? And what happened to the business in the years that followed? Those are two different questions.

Were customers actually coming back?

This is the part I find most interesting, and the hardest to answer from the filings.

Jumei spent RMB 499.1 million on marketing in 2014, RMB 655.3 million in 2015, and RMB 427.8 million in 2016. That's total marketing expense, not customer acquisition cost. Without knowing how many new customers it acquired, how often they returned, and how much profit each order generated, I can't work out a reliable CAC or customer lifetime value.

The mobile shift adds another question. By the fourth quarter of 2015, about 79.5% of Jumei's GMV came through mobile. But where did those customers come from? How many returned on their own, and how many needed another promotion?

If someone only showed up when there was a discount, how much was that customer really worth?

There's a big difference between customers who come back because they want to shop with you and customers who return because you have the best discount that week. I don't yet have the data to know which mattered more.

The 2017 detour

In 2017, Jumei invested in Jiedian, a shared power-bank company, and acquired a controlling stake. Its later filings report an 82.07% equity interest.

It's a physical business. You need devices in the right places, agreements with venues, and enough rentals to make the equipment worthwhile. That's quite different from selling cosmetics online.

Maybe management saw a better opportunity outside beauty retail. Maybe the core business had become too hard to grow. The acquisition alone doesn't tell us whether it paid off.

What I'd like to know is how management weighed it against the alternatives. Could the money have done more for the original business, or was moving into something different the better bet?

What I'm taking from it

Jumei clearly found demand. Millions of people used the platform, and it became a major name in online beauty retail.

What I'm less sure about is how much of that early success turned into something competitors couldn't easily take away.

Did customers choose Jumei because they trusted it, because it was convenient, or simply because the prices were good?

That's the part I want to understand.

Still open

  • How much of the 2016 decline was policy, and how much was competition?
  • What happened to order frequency and customer retention?
  • Did Jumei give customers a reason to stay beyond discounts?
  • Would the capital put into Jiedian have earned more in the core business?
